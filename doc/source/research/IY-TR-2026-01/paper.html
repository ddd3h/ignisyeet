Abstract

A design tool that runs CFD automatically, from CAD to force coefficients, returns numbers without saying how far to trust them. We ask whether the NASA WIND/NPARC procedure (AIAA G-077-1998) can quantify that trust for the rocket simulator IgnisYeet (the product), whose pipeline drives SU2, and if not, why not. Three layers test the solver against exact solutions, the whole chain on the same cone, and two rockets. The answer: the solver reproduces exact inviscid pressures, but the automated chain’s uncertainty was not reliably quantified. With the product’s settings SU2 reproduces the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle within 0.2 % (primary setups), yet the grid convergence index (GCI) bounded the true error in only 5 of 7 inviscid cases. Through the chain the finest level did not converge, leaving the pipeline’s own error unquantified. On the sample rocket the only usable GCI, for the Mach 2 normal-force slope, is 10.6 or 16.3 % by grid-size definition (the latter flagged); the Basic Finner has no reliable GCI, so the free-flight comparison reports sizes, not verdicts. In our judgement three obstacles stand behind this: grids the mesher cannot refine similarly, solves that stall or cycle for reasons not established, and GCIs that are conditional without an exact answer. They mark what such a tool must resolve to state a quantified uncertainty.

1 Introduction

A rocket designer who uses a tool of the kind studied here need not build a mesh. The tool takes a CAD description of the rocket, builds an unstructured volume mesh, runs a flow solver and returns force and moment coefficients, and a flight simulator turns those coefficients into a trajectory, an apogee and a landing point. IgnisYeet is such a tool (called the product below): its aerodynamic module drives the open-source solver SU2 (Economon et al. 2016) and feeds its six-degree-of-freedom flight simulation. Every step of the chain is automatic, and so is its output: a coefficient with no statement of how much to trust it.

The standard way to attach such a statement is the verification and validation procedure of the NASA Glenn WIND/NPARC project (NASA Glenn Research Center n.d.f), which follows the AIAA guide (American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics 1998). It divides the task into six steps: examination of the code, iterative convergence, consistency, spatial (grid) convergence, temporal convergence, and comparison with a more accurate solution or with measurements. Its central quantity is the grid convergence index (GCI) of Roache (1998), in the form given by Celik et al. (2008) for grids whose refinement ratio varies. The GCI turns a sequence of solutions on refined grids into a band on the finest-grid value, and that band is what makes the last step meaningful: a comparison without an uncertainty band cannot say which side is wrong.

These procedures are well established. Besides the GCI and the reporting procedure of Celik et al. (2008), the ASME standard V&V 20 (American Society of Mechanical Engineers 2009) quantifies the accuracy of a simulation against experimental data, accounting for the errors of both, Roy (2005) reviews code and solution verification, and Oberkampf and Roy (2010) treat the subject as a whole. Eça and Hoekstra (2014) extend the uncertainty estimate to grid-refinement data with scatter, by least-squares fits, but still require solutions on systematically refined grids. Design tools for small rockets can also obtain their coefficients without CFD, from semi-empirical methods: the Barrowman method (Barrowman 1967), which OpenRocket uses in extended form (Niskanen 2009), or Missile DATCOM (Blake 1998). The product instead runs CFD automatically, and it is to that case that this paper carries the procedure.

The procedure presumes that the analyst controls the grid family and the convergence of each solution: the grids are refined systematically, ideally similarly, and each solution is driven until its iterative error is small. In an automated, CAD-driven pipeline the user controls neither. Refinement is available only through the cell-size settings the tool exposes, and whether a solution has converged is judged by the tool. Applying the procedure under these constraints is therefore not routine, and it is not obvious that it can be applied at all. This paper does not survey earlier grid-convergence studies on automatically generated or unstructured meshes; its contribution is one end-to-end application of the NASA procedure to one such pipeline, and an account of where the procedure stops.

This paper asks the question directly. Can an automated CAD-to-coefficient CFD pipeline, here IgnisYeet over SU2, deliver aerodynamic coefficients with a numerical uncertainty quantified by the NASA procedure, and where it cannot, why not?

To answer it, the study separates what the solver does from what the pipeline adds and from what the application needs, in three layers (Fig. 1). Layer 0 runs SU2 directly with the numerical settings the product writes and compares it with exact solutions. Layer 1 solves one of those exact problems, a cone in supersonic flow, through the product’s whole chain, so that the difference from layer 0 would expose the error the chain adds. Layer 2 applies the tutorial’s six steps to the product at working conditions, on a sample rocket and on the Basic Finner, and compares with free-flight measurements.

The findings, each developed in one part of Section 3, are these.

  • In verification against exact solutions, SU2 with the product’s settings reproduces the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle within 0.2 % in the primary setups, but where the exact answer is known the GCI does not always bound the true error, and neither of the available checks detects both of its failures (Section 3.1).

  • The pipeline’s own error could not be quantified: the finest layer-1 level did not converge, and, independently, the product’s grids cannot be refined similarly (Section 3.2).

  • At working conditions the six steps leave almost no usable uncertainty band: one coefficient at one condition on the sample rocket, and no reliable GCI on the Basic Finner (Section 3.3).

  • The validation and model comparisons therefore report sizes of differences, not verdicts (Section 3.4).

Section 4 draws these together into an answer, ranks the obstacles behind it, separates what was shown from what was not, and states what the findings imply for such pipelines.

Terminology.

Verification asks whether the equations are solved correctly and compares a result with an exact or otherwise highly accurate solution. Cross-comparison compares one method or program with another; it shows that they differ or agree, not which is closer to reality. Validation asks whether the model resembles the real system and compares a result with measured data. Only the comparison with free-flight measurements (Section 3.4.1) is validation; layers 0 and 1 are verification.

2 Method

The product’s CFD runs made for this study use IgnisYeet v0.4.0 with SU2 8.3.0, except one coarse-level rerun with another build (Section 3.3.1); the product studies called existing below are re-evaluated from result files already committed with the product, without new CFD. This section gives only what the results need; setup details are in Appendix A.

Because the question is whether a band can be trusted, three rules decide when a number is reported at all.

  • The gate. A grid level enters a study if the solver reported convergence or, where it gave no verdict, the residual fell by at least three orders of magnitude; a level the solver reported as not converged, or one with neither piece of evidence, is refused, and the refusal states the reason.

  • The order flag. A computed order above 4 (twice the formal second order) or below 0.5 (a quarter of it) is flagged: the triplet is then outside the asymptotic range and its GCI is not reliable.

  • Oscillation. A triplet whose differences change sign (ε32/ε21<0\varepsilon_{32}/\varepsilon_{21}<0) is labelled and receives no uncertainty band. Celik et al. (2008) do compute a value for such a case; this study does not, and reports none.

Where the refinement is not geometrically similar, so that no single definition of hh is justified, the GCI is computed under every defensible definition, h∝N−1/3h\propto N^{-1/3} and h∝h\propto wall cell size, and both results are shown; the two definitions bracket the question and the spread between them is not a bound on the true GCI (Section 3.2.2).

2.3 Cases

The exact solutions of layer 0 are computed, not transcribed: the weak-solution oblique shock relation, the Taylor–Maccoll cone equation integrated from the shock to the surface with the shock angle found by shooting, the Prandtl–Meyer function, and the Blasius similarity solution (Blasius 1908; Schlichting and Gersten 2017), all for a perfect gas with γ=1.4\gamma=1.4. The oblique-shock and Prandtl–Meyer results agree with the tabulated values of the NPARC archive pages (NASA Glenn Research Center n.d.d, n.d.e) within the precision of those tables; the cone solution is checked against independent tables (Appendix A). The cases follow the NPARC archive (NASA Glenn Research Center n.d.c): a 15∘^\circ wedge and a 15∘^\circ expansion at Mach 2.5, a 10∘^\circ half-angle cone at Mach 2.35, and a laminar flat plate, run at Mach 0.2. The Blasius solution is incompressible, so a compressibility correction is estimated and reported alongside.

The layer 0 Euler runs use the numerical settings of a configuration that the product itself wrote, and a test asserts that they are identical to the product’s. The product has no laminar mode, so the laminar plate runs the Navier–Stokes equations without a turbulence model on the numerics of a product-written RANS configuration: it checks the product’s RANS discretisation with the turbulence model removed, not a configuration the product writes. Each case has three exactly nested structured levels (r=2r=2; Appendix A).

Layer 1 solves the layer 0 cone, Mach 2.35, zero incidence, Euler, as a 10∘^\circ cone-cylinder through the product on a three-dimensional unstructured half model. Its three levels have wall cell sizes of 3.92, 2.80 and 2.00 mm (a factor 1.4); the body has no fins, so the wall size is the only refinement setting. The surface pressure coefficient Cp=(p−p∞)/(12ρ∞V∞2)C_p=(p-p_\infty)/(\tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2) of the wall nodes is binned along x/Lconex/L_\mathrm{cone} and compared with the Taylor–Maccoll value 0.09673; the window 0.4≤x/Lcone≤0.90.4\le x/L_\mathrm{cone}\le0.9 is the like-for-like counterpart of layer 0’s wall window.

Layer 2 treats a sample rocket (Euler; Mach 0.3, 0.8 and 2.0; 0∘^\circ and 4∘^\circ) and the Basic Finner, a cone-cylinder with four rectangular fins, with Euler at Mach 2.375 (0∘^\circ, 2∘^\circ, 4∘^\circ; four existing levels) and with RANS (Spalart–Allmaras) at Mach 1.832 (0∘^\circ and 2∘^\circ). Mach 1.832 is chosen for RANS because it is the one point at which the product’s existing RANS validation run (wall size 1.5 mm, one grid) converged and at which free-flight data exist; at Mach 2.375 the product’s RANS stalls in a limit cycle and at Mach 3.147 it diverges. The normal-force slope is CNα=CN(4∘)/4∘C_{N\alpha}=C_N(4^\circ)/4^\circ for the sample rocket and CN(2∘)/2∘C_N(2^\circ)/2^\circ for the Basic Finner, following the existing convention, and every layer-2 family is built through the product’s own cell-size settings (Section 3.2.2).

3 Results

The results answer the question in four parts, from the solver outward: whether the solver and the GCI itself can be trusted (R1), whether the pipeline’s error can be measured (R2), which coefficients receive a band (R3), and what the comparisons can then conclude (R4).

3.1 R1. The solver reproduces exact inviscid pressures, but the GCI is not a guarantee

Layer 0 is the only place in the study where the exact answer is known. It can therefore test two things: whether SU2 with the product’s settings solves the equations correctly, and whether the GCI, which every later result depends on, bounds the true error. The first answer is yes for the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle, with exceptions for wall quantities derived from the Mach number and for boundary junctions, and the laminar plate gives no clean verification; the second is: not reliably.

In the primary setups the surface pressure and the shock angle are within 0.2 % of the exact value (Table 2); all twelve Euler runs reached the residual target (12.7 to 13.9 orders of density-residual drop), and the three laminar-plate runs dropped by 9.4 to 10.4 orders. This is the base the later layers build on, and its scope is narrow: what it verifies is SU2 with the product’s settings on structured grids, not the product’s pipeline.

3.1.2 Does the GCI bound the true error?

The GCI does not always bound the true error, and that matters because every band in the rest of the paper is a GCI computed without an exact answer. Of the seven inviscid rows with a computed GCI, the bound held for five and failed for two (Table 2): the cone wall pressure coefficient, where the true error is 3.2 times the GCI (0.188 against 0.059 %), and the same quantity in the symmetry-axis variant. “Held” does not mean tight: for the expansion wall pressure the GCI is about 8 times the error, which is a bound, not an estimate; rows flagged as outside the asymptotic range are included in the count of five.

Could the failures have been detected without the exact answer, as they would have to be in layer 2? The order flag misses both, because the cone wall pressure coefficient has p=3.39p=3.39. Stability of the order across the four wall windows catches the cone failure but misses the symmetry-axis failure, whose orders are stable, and it flags the expansion wall pressure as unstable although the GCI bounded that error. The observed orders themselves, 0.39 to 3.39 against a formal second-order scheme, are not a measurement of the scheme’s order: the wall windows contain the tip or corner transient, and the shock angle is read from a captured, smeared shock.

The laminar plate gives the same verdict for a viscous quantity. The bound held for two of five rows, and only because the sequence crosses Blasius between the medium and the fine grid, so the fine-grid CfC_f error of +0.008%+0.008\,\% is a sign crossing, not agreement; the failed rows have orders of 1.05 to 1.11, which the order flag does not catch. The difference from Blasius also mixes effects that a discretisation-error bound does not cover (Appendix A), so the laminar plate does not give a clean verification of the discretisation.

Side note: a reference table that disagrees with itself.

The NPARC archive’s table for this cone (NASA Glenn Research Center n.d.a) could not be used as it stands: its shock angle is inconsistent with its own surface Mach number and printed ratios, and three further sources, which rest on the same theory, support the angle computed here (Appendix A). For this cone only the surface Mach number and the surface pressure coefficient are compared.

What R1 establishes.

SU2 with the product’s settings reproduces the exact solutions in the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle, so the solver and its settings are verified in that sense; wall quantities derived from the Mach number and boundary junctions are the exceptions, and the laminar plate gives no clean verification. But the GCI bounded the true error in 5 of 7 inviscid and 2 of 5 laminar-plate rows, and neither the order flag nor window stability detects both inviscid failures. A GCI computed without an exact answer, which is the situation of every product result below, is therefore a conditional estimate that can understate the true error even when its order looks plausible and its windows look stable.

3.2 R2. The pipeline’s own error could not be quantified

R1 tested the solver, but layer 0 never runs the product’s pipeline. The next question is whether the chain around the solver, from CAD to an unstructured mesh to coefficients, adds an error that can be measured. It could not be measured, because the finest level did not converge, for a reason not established; and the product’s grids carry a second, structural limit that applies to every product GCI.

Even had the fine level converged, the layer-1 grids would not have been a similar family, and the same holds for every grid the product builds. The product’s refinement settings are the wall and fin cell sizes. Its mesher grades the volume mesh from the wall cell size up to a largest cell size that is one tenth of the far-field radius; that largest size is not a user setting and does not scale with the wall size, and scaling it would require changing the far-field radius, which changes the problem solved. Refining the settings therefore refines only the near-wall region. Table 4 shows the consequence for all four product families of the study: where the wall size shrinks by 1.4 per level, the count grows by 1.56 to 1.83 per level against 1.43=2.741.4^3=2.74 for a similar refinement. Neither h∝N−1/3h\propto N^{-1/3} nor h∝h\propto wall size is then the effective grid size, so every GCI of such a family is computed under both definitions, and the spread between them is treated as part of the uncertainty, not as a bound on it.

What R2 establishes.

The pipeline’s own error, including three-dimensional unstructured meshing, is not quantified. Two separate limits stand in the way: the layer-1 fine level did not converge, for a reason not established, and the product’s grids are not similar, a structural property of its mesher that would remain even if every level converged. For the part of the question that concerns the error the pipeline adds, this study can give no number.

3.3 R3. In application, almost no usable uncertainty band

With the pipeline’s error unquantified, the practical question remains: on rockets at working conditions, which coefficients does the six-step procedure equip with a band? The decisive step is spatial convergence; iterative convergence and consistency then show whether the bands that exist can be trusted.

Every other row has no band, a flagged GCI or an unusable one (Table 5): the xcpx_{cp} GCI at Mach 2 is flagged as unreliable, and CNαC_{N\alpha} at Mach 0.3 and 0.8 does not converge and receives no band, nor do xcpx_{cp} at Mach 0.3 and 0.8 (oscillatory) and CAC_A at Mach 2. No GCI of the sample rocket is therefore a certified uncertainty.

The Basic Finner Euler study at Mach 2.375 computes GCIs, but it has four existing levels and thus two triplets, L1–L3 and L2–L4, and the two disagree in their orders under both definitions (Appendix A). The only band available for the slope is therefore a sensitivity and not a GCI: the coarse triplet, whose order is flagged, gives ±0.17%\pm0.17\,\% for the plotted grid, while the largest observed change from the plotted grid to any other level, L2 to L4, is +0.47%+0.47\,\%. This four-level study does not establish a reliable GCI for the Basic Finner.

With RANS the gate, not the grids, decides. No RANS GCI was obtained at Mach 1.832: the 2∘2^\circ solve did not converge on any of the three levels, nor did the zero-incidence solve of the finest level, and the product records each as stalled, with the limit-cycle flag set. Every family is therefore refused; the slopes of the three levels, 11.52, 11.57 and 11.45, all come from not-converged solves, and their 1.1 % spread is not an uncertainty. The gate uses the smaller of the density and the Spalart–Allmaras residual drops, whereas the product’s own residual drop is that of the density alone and is larger wherever the turbulence residual fell less (on the finest level at 0∘0^\circ, 0.89 against 3.91 orders). A run of the coarse level with a different build of the product and a different domain decomposition gave the opposite verdicts at 0∘0^\circ (not converged) and 2∘2^\circ (converged); that the verdicts flip is observed, the comparison is confounded by build and decomposition, and no cause is claimed.

The same obstacle stops the product’s existing RANS grid study of the sample rocket: all four of its families are refused, on solves recorded as not converged or, at Mach 2 and 4∘4^\circ, on a rerun with no recorded verdict (Appendix A). The product’s verdict “unconverged” for a RANS case covers two kinds of solve that its documentation separates, a stall (the residual stops short of the required drop while the coefficients are flat) and a limit cycle (the residual has dropped far enough and the coefficients oscillate in a small band); a stall therefore fails the tutorial’s residual-drop condition and a limit cycle its coefficient-flatness condition, and the record does not say which kind the solve was.

3.3.2 Iterative error and consistency

The iterative error invalidates no GCI, but the check is narrow. The tutorial requires that the iterative error be small against the grid uncertainty, and of the 38 rows of the comparison no row in which the grid and the quantity match has a ratio above 1. Only one condition, however, has a usable GCI to compare with: the Mach 2 normal-force slope, whose iterative error on the finest grid is 3.2×10−53.2\times10^{-5} of the GCI. That error is a lower bound, because the reference state is the last row the solver wrote, so the result is no evidence that the iterative error disturbs this GCI but weaker than a demonstration that it is small (the other rows in Appendix A).

The consistency checks are similarly limited by what they can isolate. For a symmetric body CN(0)=0C_N(0)=0 and CN(−α)=−CN(α)C_N(-\alpha)=-C_N(\alpha) follow from the shape; on a mesh made mirror-symmetric by construction, as in the production runs of the sample rocket, the checks pass by construction and say nothing about the solver. Without mirror symmetry they fail on all 24 rows (12 of CN(0)C_N(0), 12 of the even part) of an existing asymmetry study and at Mach 0.3 and 0.8 on the sample rocket, consistent with an offset that the mesh asymmetry carries at every incidence; which part is the mesh and which the solver was not separated (Appendix A). At Mach 0.3 and zero incidence the inviscid axial force should vanish (d’Alembert’s paradox); on the three sample-rocket levels |CA||C_A| shrinks, but its extrapolation crosses the exact value, so the three grids support the trend, not a convergence to zero.

3.3.3 Code examination and temporal convergence

The remaining two steps do not change the picture. For step 1, no test of grid-convergence code existed before this study, which adds tests for the GCI procedure, the exact solutions, the identity of the layer 0 settings with the product’s and the consistency checks (Appendix A). Step 5 does not apply to the steady CFD, which has no time step. Side note: the flight integrator examined in its place shows no convergence order on the sample rocket, a spread attributed on the evidence of controls mainly to the rail model, with a second contribution not excluded (Appendix B); this lies outside the question of CFD uncertainty.

What R3 establishes.

On the sample rocket the procedure leaves one coefficient at one condition with a GCI that is neither refused nor unusable, the Mach 2 normal-force slope, and that band is wide and of poor reliability. The Basic Finner Euler study computes GCIs, but its two triplets disagree and it establishes no reliable one; with RANS the Basic Finner has none, and the product’s own published RANS grid study does not meet the procedure’s precondition (Section 3.3.1). For the question, coefficients at working conditions receive almost no usable numerical uncertainty band.

3.4 R4. Validation and model uncertainty cannot be separated from numerical uncertainty

Step 6 is where a numerical band earns its place: a difference from a measurement or from another model is attributed to the model only if it exceeds the numerical uncertainty. R3 has left almost no band, so this part asks what the comparisons can still say: the size of each difference, and in no case a verdict.

The Euler CNαC_{N\alpha} of the Basic Finner lies 2.9 to 10.5 % below the free-flight data, and whether that is model error cannot be decided. The data are the free-flight measurements of Dupuis and Hathaway (1997): aeroballistic range tests, with the coefficients obtained from a six-degree-of-freedom maximum-likelihood fit over groups of shots at eight Mach numbers between 1.077 and 4.300 (Fig. 3, Table 6). 3.4.2 Euler against RANS

None of the seven Euler-against-RANS conditions has a verdict, although the differences reach +54%+54\,\%. Comparing Euler with RANS-SA on the same condition is a cross-comparison of two models, and a difference counts as a model difference only if it exceeds the sum of the two numerical bands. Seven conditions were set against those bands, on the sample rocket at Mach 0.8 and 2 and on the Basic Finner at Mach 1.832 (conditions and differences in Appendix A). The RANS band does not exist for the sample rocket, because every triplet of that study is refused, nor for the Basic Finner, because no RANS GCI was obtained at Mach 1.832; the Euler band is missing or flagged for the sample rocket and absent for the Basic Finner at Mach 1.832, whose only Euler grid study is at Mach 2.375. The sizes are therefore reported, and whether they are a model difference or numerical error is not decided.

Side note: Spalart–Allmaras against SST.

Because the product offers one turbulence model, the comparison of models that step 6 asks for was made outside it, on a flat plate with SU2 run directly: the two models differ by −0.79%-0.79\,\% in skin friction on the fine grid, which is not separable from SST’s own GCI of 1.14 % (Appendix A). The comparison is between two models and is not validation.

What R4 establishes.

The Euler CNαC_{N\alpha} of the Basic Finner lies 2.9 to 10.5 % below the free-flight data, the Euler and RANS results differ by up to +54%+54\,\%, and the two turbulence models differ by −0.79%-0.79\,\% in skin friction; in every case the numerical band needed to call the difference a model difference is missing or flagged, or is not exceeded by the difference. Without a numerical band, the procedure’s last step can report sizes but cannot say which side is wrong.

4 Discussion

4.1 The answer

The question was whether an automated CAD-to-coefficient pipeline can deliver its coefficients with a numerical uncertainty quantified by the NASA procedure. For IgnisYeet over SU2, as it stands, the answer is: not yet, apart from one wide band of poor reliability, the only sample-rocket GCI that is neither refused nor unusable (the Mach 2 normal-force slope, 10.6 or 16.3 % on the two grid-size definitions; R3). In verification against exact solutions, SU2 with the product’s settings reproduces the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle within 0.2 % in the primary setups (R1), but the pipeline’s own error is not quantified (R2), the Basic Finner has no reliable GCI with Euler and none with RANS (R3), and the validation and model comparisons consequently report sizes and no verdicts (R4).

4.2 Three obstacles, ranked

We identify three obstacles behind this answer. We rank them by how general and how permanent each is: the first affects every product family (Table 4) and would remain if every solve converged, the second removes a GCI entirely, rather than widening it, wherever it occurs, and the third removes no band but limits what a band that survives the other two can mean. Both the attribution and the ranking are the study’s judgement from the evidence above, not a separate measurement (Section 4.3).

First, the mesher fixes the largest cell size, so the product’s grids cannot be refined similarly. Every GCI of such a family depends on a choice of grid-size definition that cannot be justified; on the sample rocket the two definitions give 10.6 and 16.3 % for the one usable GCI, and on the Basic Finner the orders of the two triplets disagree under both.

Second, solver convergence is not robust enough for the gate. The 2∘2^\circ RANS solves of the Basic Finner are recorded as stalled, with the limit-cycle flag set, on every level, the product’s RANS stalls in a limit cycle at Mach 2.375 and diverges at Mach 3.147, the existing RANS sample-rocket study is refused on solves recorded as not converged (and, at Mach 2 and 4∘4^\circ, on a rerun with no recorded verdict), and the finest layer-1 level, an Euler solve, did not converge. Its causes were not established: the dependence on rank count and decomposition is untested, and the RANS coarse level’s flipped verdicts are confounded by build and decomposition.

Third, a GCI computed without an exact answer is conditional. Layer 0 showed GCIs that understated the true error with plausible orders and, in one case, stable windows, and bounds that held were up to eight times the error.

4.3 Existence, not cause

The study shows that each obstacle exists; it does not show that an obstacle caused a particular failure. Existence was measured: the growth per level of every product family falls short of a similar refinement (Table 4), the solves that the gate refused are recorded as not converged or carry no verdict, and layer 0 has GCIs that do not bound the true error. Causation was not tested: that non-similarity makes the two Basic Finner triplets disagree, or makes the sample-rocket CNαC_{N\alpha} fail to converge at Mach 0.3 and 0.8, was not tested, and why the solves stall was not established. The answer of Section 4.1 is measured; its attribution to the three obstacles is the study’s reading of the evidence.

4.4 What the findings imply, and the next experiment

The findings point to three changes that a pipeline of this kind would need before its coefficients could carry a band. None of them was implemented or tested here; they are implications, not demonstrated remedies.

  • A global refinement control. The first obstacle implies that a GCI of the product needs a mesh control that scales the whole field, including the largest cell size, together with the wall size, so that a family can be refined similarly without changing the far-field radius. That is a change to the product, outside the scope of this study.

  • Convergence handling. The second obstacle implies that the convergence verdict should record which kind of non-convergence occurred, a stall or a limit cycle, since each fails a different condition of the tutorial’s precondition (Section 3.3.1), and that the residual drop should be measured on the weakest equation, as the gate does, rather than on the density alone.

  • Reporting the band only when the gate passes. The second obstacle, together with the published RANS grid study that does not meet the precondition, implies that a pipeline should attach a GCI only to a family whose levels pass the gate and otherwise state that no band exists; the third implies that even then the band should be labelled as a conditional estimate, with its order and oscillation flags, since layer 0 shows that the flags miss some failures.

The decisive next experiment follows from the first obstacle and from Section 4.3: give the mesher a global refinement control that scales the whole field, build a similar family on the cases of this study, and test whether its GCI then passes the gate. That would turn the first obstacle from an observed property into a tested cause, or rule it out; for the second, the dependence of the stalls on rank count and decomposition remains to be tested.

4.5 Limitations

Layer 0 verifies SU2 with the product’s settings and not the product’s pipeline. The laminar plate verifies a configuration the product does not write, and the turbulent-plate data come from a configuration close to but not identical to the product’s. The two-dimensional boundary-junction and entropy findings are untested on the three-dimensional meshes. The pipeline error was not assessed, because the finest layer 1 level did not converge. The product offers only one turbulence model, Spalart–Allmaras, so the comparison with SST was made on a flat plate with self-built grids and with SU2 directly, not through the product; SST is first order there and one of the two bands is flagged. The validation rests on a single measured data set (DREV-TM-9703), and for CX0C_{X0} the CFD has no band, so whether it overlaps the measurement cannot be judged. Two published verification references each contain an internally inconsistent entry, the NPARC 10∘^\circ cone table and one cell of Table 1 of Celik et al. (2008); that is why the reference values here are computed and checked against independent tables, and it is not a criticism of either.

5 Conclusions

An automated CAD-to-coefficient CFD tool returns coefficients whose reliability its user cannot judge. This study asked whether the NASA WIND/NPARC procedure, applied in three layers to IgnisYeet over SU2, can quantify that reliability. In verification against exact solutions, SU2 with the product’s numerical settings reproduces the inviscid surface pressure and shock angle within 0.2 % in the primary setups, but the uncertainty of the whole chain was not quantified: the pipeline’s own error could not be measured, on the sample rocket the only GCI that is neither refused nor unusable is that of the Mach 2 normal-force slope, 10.6 or 16.3 % by grid-size definition, the latter flagged, and the Basic Finner has no reliable GCI, so its comparison with free-flight data, like every Euler-against-RANS comparison, reports a size and not a verdict. For this pipeline the answer is therefore not yet, apart from that one band. The findings point, in the study’s judgement, to three obstacles, each shown to exist but none shown to cause a particular failure: grids that the mesher cannot refine similarly, solves that do not pass the convergence gate, and the conditional nature of a GCI without an exact answer; where the exact answer was known, the GCI bounded the true error for only 5 of 7 inviscid and 2 of 5 laminar-plate results, and neither the order flag nor window stability detects both failures. The findings imply, without having tested it, that a global refinement control, convergence verdicts that separate stalls from limit cycles, and a band reported only when the gate passes would be needed before such a pipeline could attach a quantified uncertainty to its coefficients.

A The GCI procedure, the grids and the secondary results

Acceptance of the GCI procedure.

The implementation reproduces the worked example of the tutorial (NASA Glenn Research Center n.d.b) (three grids, r=2r=2): it matches the printed output of the tutorial’s own program, namely the order, the extrapolated value, both GCI values and the asymptotic ratio, to a relative 10−410^{-4}. That program is single precision, so its fifth digit differs from a double-precision evaluation; the double-precision values that the tests also pin are the study’s own numbers, a regression check and not an independent published reference. Because that example has r21=r32r_{21}=r_{32}, the variable-ratio path is also tested against two monotone columns of Table 1 of Celik et al. (2008), which have unequal ratios, to the precision that table prints. One cell of that table, the extrapolated relative error in the second column, is printed as a value that differs from the value recomputed from the same table’s own printed entries; the cell is not used in the test. The third column of that table is oscillatory; the implementation refuses it by the rule of Section 2.2 rather than reproduce the table’s value. Of the product’s own tests before this study, some test the order of its time integrators, none measures the order of mesh convergence, and the CFD solver is not run by the tests in continuous integration.

Layer 0.

The numerical settings copied from the product cover the convective discretisation, reconstruction, limiter, gradient method, CFL control, linear solver and stopping criteria; the gas model is the product’s. The grids are body-fitted and structured, with three levels of 65×4965\times49, 129×97129\times97 and 257×193257\times193 nodes (85×4985\times49, 169×97169\times97, 337×193337\times193 for the plate, which has Re=105Re=10^5 per metre), exactly nested so that r=2r=2 holds exactly. In the supersonic cases the inlet, outlet and top are far-field boundaries, and every wave leaves through the outlet: along the top boundary |p/p∞−1|≤7.3×10−10|p/p_\infty-1|\le7.3\times10^{-10} in each of the twelve runs. The runs are driven to a density residual of 10−1410^{-14} or to an iteration budget; the convergence measure reported is the peak minus the final value of log⁡10\log_{10} of the density residual. B The flight integrator

The product’s time-dependent part is the six-degree-of-freedom flight integrator, and its convergence is examined by step-size refinement. The sample rocket is flown with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator with steps of 8, 4, 2 and 1 ms (r=2r=2; parachute descent, 4 m/s wind), giving two triplets, 1/2/4 ms and 2/4/8 ms, for apogee, apogee time, landing distance, flight time, rail-exit time and speed, and altitude and speed at fixed times. No order was obtained: every row is refused, as the differences change sign or two steps give identical output at its printed resolution. Over the four step sizes the apogee altitude varies by 0.0180.018 m in 4440.54440.5 m (4.1×10−64.1\times10^{-6} relative), the landing distance by 0.032 m in 7735.5 m and the flight time by 2.4 ms in 685 s; that is a spread, not an error estimate, and without a convergence order no band is claimed.

The scatter is attributed, on the evidence of controls, to the rail model: the rocket is held at rest on the rail while the thrust is below its weight, which puts a kink in the acceleration at about 2.3 ms that the integrator does not locate and that falls in a different step relative to the step ends in each run. Three controls modify the motor thrust curve (not the product’s sample): removing both the hold and the 0 to 0.05 s thrust ramp gives orders of 4.01 for apogee and 3.96 for flight time on the coarse triplet, as expected of a fourth-order scheme; removing only the hold reduces the rail-exit speed difference between the 8 and 4 ms runs from 4.5×10−44.5\times10^{-4} to 4.1×10−84.1\times10^{-8} m/s, which supports the hold as the main cause of the rail-exit scatter; and a fully nested sweep rules out the thrust-curve knots that the step does not divide as the dominant cause. A second, smaller contribution is not excluded: with only the hold removed, the coarse-triplet apogee is still non-monotone. The finest triplets of the controls stop converging at about 10−910^{-9} to 10−810^{-8} relative, a floor that persists on the nested sweep and whose cause is unidentified; the one order from a finest triplet (4.75 for the apogee time of the first control) has differences below that floor and the same run’s apogee oscillates, so it is not evidence of fourth-order convergence. This is verification of the integrator by step refinement, not a comparison with measured data.
